Photo Courtesy of Janousek Racing It’s a warm Saturday night in July. Wheat sways in the summer breeze just beyond the track entrance, a reminder that this corner of western Kansas still belongs to the prairie. Cicadas hum in the trees as the smell of race fuel and dry Kansas dirt hangs in the air. Fine dust trails behind every passing side-by-side, settling over trailers, toolboxes, and lawn chairs alike. Impact guns chatter from open trailers, broken only by the occasional holler for a wrench that has somehow grown legs and disappeared. Families laugh. Kids weave between trailers on bicycles. One by one, the grandstands begin to fill as another evening at the dirt track comes to life. Then the loudspeaker crackles. “ Attention in the pits… Rookie Karts to the staging area. Rookie Karts to staging. ” Immediately, parents make their final checks. Helmet straps are tightened. Gloves are adjusted. Tiny engines sputter to life as nervous young drivers inch toward the staging lane. Nine-year-old Sammy has been waiting for this moment since eight o’clock that morning. Now he’s sitting on the pole, eyes locked on the track ahead. Back at the trailer, Prycen isn’t headed to staging just yet. He’s making one last adjustment before the Open division is called. As one of the youngest drivers in the class, he’ll line up beside racers with years more experience. Once the green flag waves, though, age doesn’t matter. Preparation does. Confidence does. Watching the two of them on the same race night tells the story of dirt-track racing better than any history book ever could. Sammy is just getting started. Prycen is beginning to make a name for himself. Everybody starts somewhere, and on any given Saturday night, you’ll find racers at every stage of the journey. Sammy and Prycen preparing for race night. Photo courtesy of Jason Dody. Long before there were Rookie Karts, Modifieds, or Late Models, there was a simple question. “How fast will it go?” Truth be told, people have been asking that question for centuries. We raced horses across open fields. We sailed ships across oceans. We climbed mountains simply because they were there. It was only a matter of time before someone looked at one of those newfangled automobiles, grinned at the fellow beside him, and said, “I bet mine’s faster.” The first racers didn’t have purpose-built speedways waiting for them. They used horse tracks, county fairgrounds, and dusty little ovals because that’s what they had. Before long, crowds started showing up just to see whose car was the fastest. Families packed the grandstands. Mechanics spent late nights chasing one more horsepower. Drivers spent all week thinking about Saturday night and all winter thinking about next season. The cars have changed. The feeling hasn’t. As the sport grew, someone finally admitted it could probably use a few rules. In 1915, the International Motor Contest Association, better known as IMCA, was founded to help organize a rapidly growing pastime.
